    Identify:¶

    Why do we need to color sort?¶

    Our bot's scoring and storage mechanism is 2 chambers. We chose to use 2 chambers because it has a few advantages over a regular, single chamber lever bot. One of these benefits is simply more storage space. The other main benefit, however, excites me more; having 2 separate chambers allows us to separate the red blocks from the blue blocks.

    Any bot without a color sorting mechanism runs the risk of scoring points for the opponent and their options are limited to: avoid the wrong color as much as possible, carefully try to score their own color while trying to remove the opposing color, or just give up and score the opponent some points.

    A bot with a color sorting system can simply pick up blocks and not worry about the bad ones. This saves a lot of time and does not score points for the opponent.

    Not all lever bot designs that want to color sort need 2 barrels! In fact, I have not seen a single lever bot with 2 barrels, but I have seen plenty that can color sort. Most bots, lever or otherwise, simply eject the wrong color during or just after intaking. We considered this as well. Ignoring the benefit of increased storage, there is still another advantage our double chamber has over regular color sorting; The ability to keep the opposite color.

    Why would we want to keep the wrong color? Perhaps we want to stop the other team from getting more blocks? No, It is too hard to monopolize blocks, too easy to descore, and 5-7 blocks wouldn't make much of a difference. Perhaps we want to use them to strategically position our blocks in the control zones? No, a single lever design that could adjust the block lineup would be better suited for that. Besides, that gives points to the other side, it is just as easy to pick up more of our own blocks. There appears to be no logical reason to want to hold on to your opponent's blocks in match! That is because there is not a good enough reason in a match to justify a double chamber design. The reason comes not from a match, but from the skills challenge.

    This is the is scoring for the skills challenge:

    Category Points
    Each Block Scored in a Goal 1 Point
    Each filled Control Zone in a Long Goal 5 Points
    Each filled Control Zone in a Center Goal 10 Points
    Each Cleared Park Zone 5 Points
    Each Cleared Loader 5 Points
    Parked Robot 15 Points
    There is 2 long goals and 2 center goals. That is 30 points for just the goals (not including the points for the individual blocks). The rule book explains these 2 categories as follows:

    "A Control Zone is considered filled if it ends the Match containing its maximum number of Scored Blocks, and if all Blocks in that Control Zone are the same color (e.g., all are blue, or all are red). - i. In a Robot Skills Match, a Center Goal Control Zone must contain at least seven (7) Blocks to be considered filled. - ii. A Long Goal must include one or more of the following to be considered filled in a Robot Skills Match: 1. Three Blocks of the same color fully contained between the inner edges of the Control Zone tape lines. 2. Four Blocks of the same color fully contained between the outer edges of the Control Zone tape lines."

    This means that in order to get these 30 points from control zones, all blocks in the control zone needs to be the same color. For a bot without color sorting, these 30 points are very difficult to get. With a regular color sorting bot, it becomes a little easier. But with our bot separates and keeps both colors, making this much easier. This allows us to in theory get very good skills scores, both autonomous and driver control.

    • It needs to fit in the bot (needs to be small and compact)
    • It needs to accurately tell if there is a red block, blue block, or no block

    • It needs to be be able choose which chamber the block needs to go to and move it accordingly
    • It needs to work with the intake Semi optional (but not really):
    • Spit blocks back out the intake for bottom goal

    Speed Calculations (Revised With Physical Queue + Sensor Constraints)¶

    Intake Speed Reality Check¶

    At 70 in/s intake speed, treating blocks as spheres with diameter ~3.625 in:

    \[ \text{Blocks/sec} = \frac{70\text{ in}}{\text{sec}} \cdot \frac{1\text{ block}}{3.625\text{ in}} = 19.3\text{ blocks/sec} \]

    This tells us the intake speed is not the bottleneck.

    Sorting reliability matters more than raw throughput.

    Because blocks will physically queue at the sorter entrance if not processed fast enough, intake speed and sorting speed are decoupled. Once a block reaches the queue point, it effectively “waits,” removing the hard timing constraint that exists in fly-by sorting.

    Sorter Cycle Time Requirement¶

    If blocks arrive every 150 ms (≈6.7 blocks/sec worst case), the sorter must complete a divert cycle within that window.

    Required RPM for a 2/3 rotation cam move:

    \[ \text{RPM} = \frac{2/3\text{ rotation}}{150\text{ ms}} \cdot \frac{1000}{1} \cdot \frac{60}{1} \approx 267\text{ RPM} \]

    With 1:2 gearing, the 5.5W motor provides 400 RPM, giving ample margin even with conservative acceleration.

    Sensor Speed and Queued Blocks¶

    Optical Sensor Refresh Reality¶

    • Nominal refresh: ~100 ms
    • Effective polling (manual reads): 5–20 ms

    Max reliable reads/sec (manual polling):

    \[ \frac{1}{10\text{ ms}} = 100\text{ reads/sec} \]

    However, without queuing, a block moving at 70 in/s would only be visible for:

    \[ \frac{3.25\text{ in}}{70\text{ in/s}} = 46\text{ ms} \]

    This is not enough to guarantee a read if the sensor refresh and block arrival are out of phase.

    Effect of Physical Queuing (Critical Change)¶

    Because blocks physically stack and cannot enter the sort zone while another block is present:

    • The sensed block is stationary
    • The optical sensor can take multiple reads
    • Color confidence becomes deterministic rather than probabilistic

    This eliminates the “missed block” failure mode entirely.

    Sensor Placement Constraints¶

    • Space is extremely cramped
    • Max usable lead distance: ~3–4 inches
    • Distance sensor must share mounting location with optical sensor
    • Ambient light enters from the front and sides

    Ultra-Black Paint (Legality + Function)¶

    Ultra-black paint (≈99.4% absorption):

    • Suppresses ambient lighting
    • Creates near-zero noise floor for brightness detection without much effort
    • Allows single-threshold triggering instead of averaging

    Legality:
    Per R20a, materials used strictly as a color marker for a legal sensor are allowed. This qualifies.

    Regular matte black (~95%) is acceptable if ultra-black is unavailable.

    Sensor Processing Logic (Queued Mode)¶

    Because the block is stationary while queued, the logic can be reactive instead of predictive.

    State Machine¶

    • IDLE: Distance < threshold → block present
    • SENSE: Take 2–3 color reads (brightness wake-up)
    • ACTUATE: Snap cam to target (±120°)
    • READY: Hold cam, wait for intake pulse
    • RELEASE: Intake advances block into biased paddles
    • RESET: Wait until distance clears

    Brightness Threshold (Fast Path)¶

    • Brightness < 10% → background
    • Brightness > 30% → block present

    This avoids slow RGB math unless necessary.

    System Latency Budget (Reinterpreted)¶

    Component Time
    Optical sensing (queued) 10–15 ms
    Code execution ~3 ms
    Motor acceleration ~2–3 ms
    Cam travel (1:2 gearing) ~75 ms
    Total (non-critical) ~90 ms

    Because the block is waiting, this latency no longer needs to fit inside the travel time window.

    Motor and Inertia Analysis¶

    CAD-Derived Properties (Full Mechanism)¶

    • Mass: 2.22 oz (63 g)
    • Center of mass nearly on axis
    • \(I_{zz} = 1.498\ \text{oz·in}^2\)

    Acceleration Reality¶

    Converted inertia: $$ I_{zz} = 2.74 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{kg·m}^2 $$

    The 5.5W motor can spin this mass to speed in ~2–3 ms.
    Acceleration is negligible; RPM is the true limiter.

    Gearing Comparison (Final)¶

    Gearing Torque @ Cam 180° Time Required Lead
    1:1 1.55 lbs 150 ms ~10.5"
    1:2 0.77 lbs 75 ms ~5"
    1:3 0.5 lbs 50 ms ~3.5"

    Final Selection: 1:2 Gearing¶

    • Enough torque to push against queued blocks
    • Fast enough to feel “instant”
    • Safest balance for a 5.5W motor

    1:3 is rejected due to stall risk under spring load.

    Spring and Cam Design Implications¶

    • The motor is fighting springs, not inertia
    • Add flat spots at cam peaks to prevent back-drive
    • Neutral cam position holds paddles open for easy entry

    Force Budget @ 1:2¶

    • Torque: 2.3 lb-in
    • Lever arm: 3"
    • Available force: 0.77 lbs

    Action item:
    If paddle movement requires >0.5 lbs, springs must be softened.

    Failure Recovery Logic¶

    • If motor current spikes >2.0A:
    • Briefly reverse cam 20°
    • Retry snap
    • If color confidence fails after 5 reads:
    • Default to safe path (dump or majority color)

    Select:¶

    After revisiting mechanical feasibility, sensor reliability, timing constraints, and integration with the intake, the following selections are finalized for the color sorting system.

    Sorting Method¶

    Rotation and Actuation¶

    • Actuation Method: Cam-driven rotation using standoff push points
    • Pivot Style: Collar-based central pivot
    • Material Choice: Steel V-walls for stiffness and repeatable geometry

    This combination eliminates sliding-slot wear, reduces tolerance stackup, and allows the cam and pivot to share a common axis.

    Motor and Gearing¶

    • Motor: 5.5W VEX motor
    • Gearing: **1:2 reduction

    Justification:

    • Provides sufficient torque margin against spring load and queued blocks
    • Achieves sub-100 ms actuation without approaching stall conditions
    • Best balance of speed, reliability, and mechanical forgiveness

    Sensor Placement¶

    Due to space constraints, the sensor can only either look at the floor ~3 inches ahead or at the cam walls ~0.5–1 inch away (painted with ~99.5% light-absorbing black).

    Selected: cam-wall–referenced sensing

    Looking at the cam walls provides a controlled, ultra-dark reference surface, giving far higher contrast and repeatability than floor sensing. Because blocks physically queue and remain stationary, the system can take multiple reads.

    This design intentionally prioritizes accuracy over speed. With queued blocks, sorting does not need to happen in a fly-by window, allowing slower, higher-confidence sensing without impacting throughput.

    Floor-looking sensing is therefore rejected due to inconsistent lighting and weaker color confidence.
